\begin{frame}{From data type to data structure}
\begin{itemize}
\item Class 13 asked whether data is tabular, time series, spectral, or image.
\item This class asks an orthogonal question: how organized is it, structured, semi-structured, or unstructured?
\item Any of the four data types from Class 13 can appear in any of these three organizational forms.
\item The organizational form determines how much encoding work is needed before a model can use the data at all.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Representations: encoding and embedding}
\begingroup\small
A representation is a map $\phi: \mathcal{V} \to \mathbb{R}^{k}$ that gives each raw value $v$ a numerical vector; every model operates on $\phi(v)$, never on $v$. Two families:
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.6em}
\begingroup\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
& \bfseries Encoding & \bfseries Embedding \\
\toprule
Construction & Fixed a priori by the analyst & Estimated from data by optimizing an objective \\
\addlinespace[0.3em]
Dimension & Set by the scheme ($K$ for one-hot, 1 or 2 otherwise) & Chosen by the analyst, typically $k \ll K$ \\
\addlinespace[0.3em]
Geometry & No similarity unless designed & Similar values map to nearby vectors \\
\addlinespace[0.3em]
Typical use & Low-cardinality nominal, ordinal, cyclic & High-cardinality categories, text, images \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.6em}
{\footnotesize Neither family compresses by definition: one-hot expands one column into $K$.\par}
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Encodings}
\begingroup\small
An encoding is a map $\phi_{\mathrm{enc}}: \mathcal{V} \to \mathbb{R}^{k}$ whose form is specified before training. One-hot, ordinal, and cyclic encodings are fully determined by the analyst; target encoding also estimates one statistic per value from the training labels. The choice depends on the cardinality $K = |\mathcal{V}|$ and the measurement scale of the variable.
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.4em}
\begingroup\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries}l l c >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
Encoding & $\phi_{\mathrm{enc}}(v)$ & $k$ & Appropriate when \\
\toprule
One-hot & $\mathbf{e}_v \in \{0,1\}^{K}$ & $K$ & Nominal scale, small $K$ \\
Ordinal & $r(v) \in \{1, \dots, K\}$, $r$ order-preserving & $1$ & Ordinal scale: the order is meaningful \\
Target & $\hat{\mu}_v = \dfrac{n_v \bar{y}_v + m\,\bar{y}}{n_v + m}$ & $1$ & Nominal scale, large $K$; estimated out-of-fold to avoid target leakage \\
Cyclic & $\big(\sin(2\pi v/P),\ \cos(2\pi v/P)\big)$ & $2$ & Periodic variables with period $P$ (e.g.\ hour of day, $P = 24$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.4em}
{\scriptsize $n_v$: number of training samples with value $v$; $\bar{y}_v$: their mean target; $\bar{y}$: global mean; $m \ge 0$: smoothing weight, which shrinks $\hat{\mu}_v$ towards $\bar{y}$, more strongly when $n_v$ is small.\par}
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why encoding exists}
\begin{itemize}
\item Models compute with numbers; most raw variables (categories, labels, identifiers) are not numbers.
\item Encoding is the deliberate, documented choice of how a symbolic value becomes a numeric one.
\item A bad encoding can inject a false sense of order, leak information, or blow up dimensionality.
\item The right choice depends on cardinality (how many distinct values) and semantics (does order mean anything).
\end{itemize}
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Target encoding: a worked example}
\begingroup\small
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{\color{courseblue} 1. Category means.} Each value $v$ is replaced by the mean target of the training samples with that value (Titanic, $y$ = survived).
\vspace{0.4em}

\begingroup\footnotesize\centering
\begin{tabular}{c r r c}
Port $v$ & $n_v$ & $\sum y$ & $\bar{y}_v$ \\
\toprule
C & 168 & 93 & 0.554 \\
Q & 77 & 30 & 0.390 \\
S & 644 & 217 & 0.337 \\
\midrule
All & 889 & 340 & $\bar{y} = 0.382$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.4em}
{The nominal column becomes a single numerical column, whatever the cardinality $K$; one-hot encoding would require $K$ columns.}

\column{0.5\textwidth}
\textbf{\color{courseblue} 2. Smoothing.} Rare values yield unreliable means; $\hat{\mu}_v$ is a weighted average of $\bar{y}_v$ and $\bar{y}$:
\[
\hat{\mu}_v = \frac{n_v \bar{y}_v + m\,\bar{y}}{n_v + m}, \qquad m = 50.
\]
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begingroup\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}
\item Port C ($n_v = 168$): $\hat{\mu}_C = \frac{93 + 50 \cdot 0.382}{218} = 0.514$.
\item Hypothetical port ($n_v = 2$, both survived): $\bar{y}_v = 1.0$, but $\hat{\mu}_v = \frac{2 + 19.1}{52} = 0.41$.
\end{itemize}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.3em}
\textbf{\color{courseblue} 3. Leakage control.} If a sample's own $y_i$ enters its encoding, the feature reveals the target. Training samples are therefore encoded out-of-fold; test samples use the means estimated on the training set.
\end{columns}
\par\endgroup
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{High cardinality and unseen values}
\begingroup\small
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.5\textwidth}
\textbf{\color{courseblue} Further encodings for large $K$}
\vspace{0.3em}

\begingroup\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{>{\bfseries}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X}
Count & $\phi(v) = n_v$ (or $n_v/n$), $k = 1$. Unsupervised, so no target leakage; values with equal counts become indistinguishable. \\
Hashing & $\phi(v) = \mathbf{e}_{h(v) \bmod B} \in \{0,1\}^{B}$, $k = B$ fixed whatever $K$. Unseen values need no special handling; distinct values can collide, and the map cannot be inverted, so interpretability is lost. \\
Target & $\hat{\mu}_v$, $k = 1$ (previous slide). Supervised; requires out-of-fold estimation. \\
\end{tabularx}
\par\endgroup

\column{0.46\textwidth}
\textbf{\color{courseblue} Values not seen in training}
\vspace{0.3em}

\begingroup\footnotesize
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{3pt}
\item \textbf{One-hot:} all-zero vector (\texttt{handle\_unknown="ignore"}) or a shared ``infrequent'' column (\texttt{min\_frequency} in scikit-learn).
\item \textbf{Target:} with $n_v = 0$ the smoothed formula gives $\hat{\mu}_v = \bar{y}$, so the fallback is built in.
\item \textbf{Ordinal:} no safe default; the position of a new level must be decided explicitly.
\item \textbf{Embedding:} a reserved row for unknown values ($\langle$\texttt{UNK}$\rangle$), trained on the rarest values.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.2em}
Rare values should be grouped before fitting: their encodings and embeddings are estimated from few samples.
\par\endgroup
\end{columns}
\par\endgroup
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Embeddings}
\begingroup\small
An embedding is a learned map $\mathbf{z} = E^{\top}\phi_{\mathrm{enc}}(v)$, with $E \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times k}$ and $k \ll K$: each row of $E$ is the dense vector assigned to one value, estimated from data jointly with a model or through a dedicated objective (e.g.\ word2vec for text).
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.3em}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.56\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/class14_encoding_vs_embedding.pdf}

\vspace{0.2em}
\begingroup\scriptsize\raggedright
One-hot encoding treats all categories as equidistant; the embedding places phases with similar sensor behaviour close together (HeatUp and Cool; Idle and Discharge). Embedding obtained by principal component analysis (PCA) of the standardized phase-wise means of nine sensor variables over 100 batches simulated with \texttt{synth\_sensors.py}.
\par\endgroup

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{PCA intuition}
PCA rotates the original feature space to new orthogonal axes called principal components, ordered by the amount of variance they explain.
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{itemize}
\item The first component captures the largest possible variance; later components capture what is left, subject to orthogonality.
\item Keeping only the leading components compresses the representation while trying to preserve most of the structure.
\item PCA must be fit only on training data, then applied identically at inference time.
\end{itemize}
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Scores, loadings, and latent space}
\begingroup\small
For centred (here also standardized) data $X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$, PCA with $k$ components gives
\[
T = X_c W_k \quad \text{(encoder)}, \qquad \hat{X}_c = T W_k^{\top} \quad \text{(decoder)}, \qquad W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times k},\ W_k^{\top} W_k = I.
\]
\vspace{-0.6em}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Loadings} $W_k$: each column is the direction of one component, written in the original variables (the arrows of the biplot). They live in the space of the variables.
\item \textbf{Scores} $T$: the coordinates of each sample on the components (the points of the biplot).
\item \textbf{Latent space}: the $k$-dimensional space in which the scores live; PC1--PC2 spans a 2D latent space, reported with its explained variance.
\item \textbf{Embedding}: the score vector of one object, i.e.\ its point in the latent space.
\end{itemize}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.2em}
\begingroup\scriptsize
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item The loadings make linear latent axes interpretable (next slides: a temperature axis and a flow axis); neural encoders offer no such direct reading.
\item ``Loadings'' denotes either the unit eigenvectors $W$ or $W\Lambda^{1/2}$ (for standardized data, the correlations between variables and components): state which one is reported.
\item Probabilistic PCA, $\mathbf{x} = W\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, I)$, makes $\mathbf{z}$ a latent variable in the statistical sense (Tipping and Bishop, 1999).
\end{itemize}
\par\endgroup
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two uses of PCA in this class}
\begingroup\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.2\textwidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X r >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X r}
Use & Rows of the input & $n$ & A point is & PC1 + PC2 \\
\toprule
Dimensionality reduction & Sensor samples $\mathbf{x}_t$ (20 batches) & 6{,}362 & One observation, in the latent space of the samples & $30\% + 21\%$ \\
Embedding of a categorical variable & Phase-wise means $\mathbf{m}_v$ (100 batches) & 6 & One value of the variable \texttt{state} & $49\% + 23\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.8em}
\begingroup\small
\begin{itemize}
\item Same algorithm, different objects: the first projects observations, the second represents the categories of a variable.
\item The percentages are not comparable. In the samples, PCA must also explain the variability within each phase (noise, transients); averaging removes it, so the differences between phases dominate the variance of the means.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Embedding with PCA: a worked example (1/3)}
\begingroup\small
\textbf{Goal:} a $k = 2$ embedding of the $K = 6$ process phases, learned from sensor data (100 batches simulated with \texttt{synth\_sensors.py}).
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.3em}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.45\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/class14_pca_embedding_step12.pdf}

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
\item \textbf{Step 1:} each phase $v$ is described by the mean of nine sensor variables over its samples (row $\mathbf{m}_v$ of $M$).
\item \textbf{Step 2:} each sensor $j$ is standardized, $(m_{vj} - \mu_j)/\sigma_j$, where $\mu_j$ and $\sigma_j$ are the mean and standard deviation of sensor $j$ over all samples, so that variables in different units (°C, mS/cm, cP) become comparable. Because the phases have different durations, the phase means are not centred after this step; each column is therefore centred on the mean of the six phases, so that $0$ represents the average phase.
\end{itemize}
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Embedding with PCA: a worked example (2/3)}
\begingroup\small
\textbf{Step 3:} PCA searches for new axes, each a weighted sum of the nine columns of $Z_c$, along which the phases differ as much as possible.
\par\endgroup
\vspace{0.3em}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth,height=0.38\textheight,keepaspectratio]{images/class14_pca_embedding_step3.pdf}

\vspace{0.2em}
\begin{itemize}\footnotesize
\item \textbf{3a:} each bar is the share of the total variation between phases explained by one axis. The first two axes explain $49\% + 23\% = 72\%$, so we keep $k = 2$. PC6 explains $0\%$ by construction: the six centred rows sum to zero, so at most five axes carry variance. With so few points, the shares are fragile estimates.
\item \textbf{3b:} the bars are the weights (loadings) of each sensor in the new axes.
$z_1$ weights temperature positively ($+0.58$) and viscosity $\eta$ negatively ($-0.56$): it is high for hot, thin milk, i.e.\ a \emph{temperature axis}.
$z_2$ weights inflow negatively and outflow and conductivity positively: it separates filling from discharge, i.e.\ a \emph{flow axis}.
pH and P receive weight $\approx 0$: they do not change between phases. Unlike the axes of word2vec, these axes can be read through their loadings.
\end{itemize}
\coursefooter
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Embedding with PCA: a worked example (3/3)}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.47\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{images/class14_pca_embedding_step4.pdf}
\column{0.51\textwidth}
\begingroup\small
\textbf{Step 4:} the coordinates of a phase are the weighted sums of its values in $Z_c$ (step 2, heatmap) with the weights of step 3b. For Hold:
\par\endgroup
\vspace{-0.2em}
\begingroup\footnotesize
\[
\begin{aligned}
z_1 &= \underbrace{0.58 \cdot 2.01}_{T} + \underbrace{(-0.56)(-1.98)}_{\eta} + \underbrace{0.34 \cdot 0.73}_{\tau} + \dots \\
&= 1.16 + 1.11 + 0.25 + \dots = 2.85
\end{aligned}
\]
\par\endgroup
\begingroup\scriptsize
Repeating this for every phase and for $z_2$ gives $E = Z_c W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times 2}$, the matrix $E$ of the embedding definition: row $v$ is $\mathbf{z}_v = E^{\top}\mathbf{e}_v$.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item Close points mean similar sensor profiles.
\item Hold lies far right: the hottest phase.
\item Fill is isolated: the only phase with inflow.
\item HeatUp and Cool coincide: they differ only in dT/dt, which lies on PC3 (17\%) and is discarded with $k = 2$. This is the cost of compression.
\end{itemize}
\par\endgroup
\end{columns}
